# Chapter 6 — A training step, stretched across time

## One step contains a crowd of unfinished work

At a small scale, it is tempting to imagine a tidy cycle: sample prompts, finish all responses, score them, update the model. MiMo-V2.6’s setting is less tidy. About 25,000 long trajectories are generated per global batch, many through tools and environments with different runtimes. Some finish quickly; others continue for a long time. If the trainer waits for the slowest rollout, accelerator capacity sits idle. [§4.1, pp. 8–9; §6.3, pp. 29–31]

The authors use **asynchronous partial rollout**. When a training batch is ready, unfinished sequences can be paused and resumed in a later rollout phase. This keeps work moving, but it has a cost: after the policy updates, a continued trajectory needs a new prefill and KV cache. Large batches help amortize that extra work. They also allow a global policy update to use a large volume of data. [§4.1, p. 9]

## Why filter groups that are all the same?

GRPO compares multiple samples for one prompt. If every sample in a group passes, or every one fails, the group may provide little useful relative signal. The dynamic sampler filters these all-pass and all-fail groups so the training batch is made of groups with informative outcomes. This improves gradient usefulness, but creates a sampling complication: filtering rates differ by task, so the raw prompt mixture is not the final training mixture. [§4.1, p. 9; §6.3, pp. 29–30]

## Staleness: asynchronous does not mean arbitrary

In asynchronous RL, a trajectory can be generated by a slightly older policy than the one currently being trained. The paper sets partial-rollout staleness to 4 in the main run and records rollout probabilities for importance sampling. More generally, the authors align inference and training behavior with routing replay and sampling-candidate replay, covered in Chapter 18. [§5.1, pp. 20–21]

This is the core tradeoff: asynchronous execution increases utilization, while stale samples and changed policy versions can weaken the relation between the behavior policy and the optimized policy. The importance ratio corrects for distribution change within bounds; it cannot make unlimited staleness harmless.
