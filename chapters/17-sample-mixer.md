# Chapter 17 — Keeping a mixed-task batch mixed

## A target percentage is not an actual percentage

Suppose a trainer asks for equal contributions from code and general workflows. Code rollouts may finish quickly and pass often; general tasks may run much longer and be rejected more often by a dynamic sampler. If the system simply launches the same number of prompts from each source, the final training batch can skew sharply. Across 25 profiled data sources, the paper reports up to 90× variation in mean generated tokens and 66× variation in active rollout duration. [§6.3, pp. 29–30, Fig. 15]

The **Sample Mixer** has four mechanisms: adaptive rollout concurrency, adaptive scheduling, predictive dispatch, and sample replay. Together they aim to deliver the specified training distribution despite different task runtimes and acceptance rates. [§6.3, p. 29]

## The scheduling intuition

For source \(i\), let \(B_i\) be the desired number of accepted groups per training step, \(r_i\) its acceptance rate, and \(t_i\) its active rollout duration. The expected number of generated groups required is roughly \(m_i=B_i/r_i\). A source with low acceptance needs more attempts; a slow source needs more concurrent work. The paper adapts per-source oversampling and budgets using recent estimates rather than assuming every source has the same speed. [§6.3, pp. 29–30, Eq. (6)]

Scheduling then balances two needs: keep generating enough data from a source over time, and prioritize sources that are still behind on the current batch. In a trace-driven simulation, a deficit-corrected policy (mixing both needs) improves occupancy stability versus deficit-only scheduling and collection balance versus target-only scheduling. This is a simulation on a profiled workload, not a universal proof that one fixed coefficient is optimal. [§6.3, pp. 30–31, Eqs. (7), Fig. 16]

## Predict, admit, recover

Predictive dispatch estimates a rollout’s total KV-cache demand and chooses a rank with sufficient capacity and room in its running-request budget. The estimate learns from completed rollouts. Sample replay helps at startup or recovery: because slow sources contribute fewer early completions, the system can reuse recent completed groups for the first collection step, subject to policy-staleness limits and current filtering rules. [§6.3, p. 31]

These are not peripheral optimizations. If a task’s actual share changes when another task gets slower, the experiment’s training mixture changes too. Reliable scheduling is therefore part of experimental validity.
